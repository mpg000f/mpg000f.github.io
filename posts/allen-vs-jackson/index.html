Quarterback analysis · 2018–2025

Josh vs Lamar.
A Deep Dive.

If you’ve ever been on NFL Twitter, you’ve likely encountered furious Baltimoreans and Buffalonians heatedly defending the honor of their respective quarterbacks, sometimes going so far as to resort to racially charged insults. Let’s scale back the rhetoric and dive into cold, hard stats.

By Salamander Jefferson · computed from nflverse play-by-play · complete through Super Bowl LX

They both came into the league in the first round of the 2018 draft, alongside Baker Mayfield, Sam Darnold and Josh Rosen. I think it’s safe to say they’re the obvious top two quarterbacks in that class. And in the past couple of years — since Josh won a very close vote for 2024 MVP but Lamar was still named the first-team All-Pro quarterback — it’s not hyperbole to say their fans view it as the preeminent rivalry in the league.

What follows is a statistical deep dive of their production — every regular-season and playoff snap either has taken since 2018, through Super Bowl LX — including trailing six- and three-year windows, and stacking all of their individual seasons against each other. Along the way I tackle two of the narratives that get flung around in the debate: that Josh feasts on wild-card weekend, and that his rushing production is a product of vulturing James Cook touchdowns. Neither holds up especially well. I hope you enjoy.

Act one · the whole career

Over eight years, it is close

Each row below carries two numbers: the career total on the outside, and the per-game rate underneath it. The rate is the one worth comparing, because Allen has played ten more games than Jackson. Everything in this section is regular season only — the playoffs get their own act further down, and they do not read the same way.

The division of labor is roughly what you would expect. Allen throws for about 44 more yards a game, which over a seventeen-game season is somewhere near 740 yards. Jackson runs for about 19 more, or a little over 300 yards across the same season — a margin that has narrowed as Baltimore has leaned on him less as a runner.

The two columns that decide most arguments are touchdowns and turnovers, and they point in opposite directions. Jackson is the more careful quarterback: 0.73 turnovers a game to Allen’s 0.98, which across a full season comes to about four fewer giveaways. Allen scores more: 2.39 total touchdowns a game to Jackson’s 1.91, or roughly eight more touchdowns over those same seventeen games. Neither margin is a rounding error, and which of the two you weigh more heavily is close to the whole disagreement.

EPA — expected points added — is the more analytical way to compare them, and some would happily call it a nerd stat. All it does is score every snap by how much it moved the team toward or away from points, so nine yards on third-and-eight is worth far more than the same nine yards on third-and-ten. One converts, the other punts.

By that measure both of them are very good, which is what makes it an interesting comparison rather than a settled one: +0.194 per play for Allen, +0.180 for Jackson across the eight years. A real gap, but a narrow one.

The boards below are there for reference, to show who else lives in that neighborhood. Allen sits second in total EPA over the span and Jackson third, and only Patrick Mahomes has clearly separated from either of them. What the career figure hides is that it averages two completely different stretches — which is where the next two sections go.

One thing worth sitting with before moving on. The per-play gap between them is small, but the total is not. Allen’s +1038.0 to Jackson’s +767.3 is a margin of 271 points of expected value. Availability counts. So does what they do with the plays they get.

Allen has taken 1,093 more qualifying snaps over the eight years. Value those extra plays at Jackson’s career EPA per play and they explain about 73% of the gap. The rest comes from Allen’s higher rate. He has simply been on the field more: 126 games to Jackson’s 116.

Act two · 2018–2019

Lamar’s dominant start

Averaging eight seasons together buries the single most lopsided stretch of this rivalry, and it is the part of the argument Baltimore fans are right about. Start with the raw stats, though, because they do not look like a blowout.

Over 2018 and 2019 Jackson scored more touchdowns, 54 to 47. He ran for far more, 1,901 yards to 1,141. And he took care of the ball at roughly twice the rate: 15 turnovers to Allen’s 27, or 0.48 a game against 0.96. Allen, for his part, actually threw for more yards — 5,133 to 4,328, and in three fewer games.

On a per-game basis it reads closer still. Their touchdown rates are nearly identical, 1.74 for Jackson to 1.68 for Allen. If counting stats were the whole story you would call these two roughly even with different styles, one throwing and one running, and the only clear separation would be the turnovers.

The efficiency gap is where you actually see it, and it is enormous. Jackson posted +0.245 expected points per play to Allen’s +0.031. Over those two years Jackson ranked third in the league in both EPA per play and total EPA. Allen ranked 31st of 39 qualified quarterbacks by rate and 26th by total. In total value it was +226.7 to +32.9. Look at 2019 on its own and it is starker still: Jackson led the league in both measures, while Allen finished 21st in both.

That is the difference between an elite signal caller piloting an offense and a young quarterback still learning how to play in the league. Allen was completing 56% of his passes and throwing 21 interceptions across those two years, piloting a mediocre-at-best Buffalo offense that ranked 27th in EPA per play and 30th in scoring over the stretch. Some of that is on him and some of it is what he had to work with.

Jackson had no such excuse to need. He took over Baltimore midway through his rookie season, made the playoffs, and won a unanimous MVP in year two, running an offense that ranked 3rd in EPA per play and fourth in scoring across the same two seasons. Jackson was a star before Allen was average, and those two seasons stay on the ledger.

Act three · the recent windows

Since then, Allen has been the better quarterback

Drop those first two seasons and the picture inverts. Start again with the traditional stuff, because it is where the change is most obvious.

Since 2020 Allen has thrown for 24,939 yards, third-most of any quarterback in the league over the span, and run for 3,580, second-most. Put those together and he is second in total yards among quarterbacks, behind only Patrick Mahomes — and by 173 yards across six seasons. Jackson is 12th in passing yards and first in rushing, which nets out to sixth in total yards.

Touchdowns are the cleaner separation. Allen has scored 254 over the last six years, more than any quarterback in football. Jackson has 168, eighth. Narrow it to the last three and nothing moves: Allen is first in touchdowns again and second in total yards, Jackson fifth and sixth. Allen has led all quarterbacks in total touchdowns in every window this article looks at, including the full eight years.

The one traditional measure that does not follow the pattern is the touchdown-to-turnover ratio, which is really the turnover edge wearing a different hat. Over the last six years Allen is ahead there too, 2.65 to 2.40, but narrow to the last three and Jackson takes it back, 3.03 to 2.88. It is the one column where he is reliably competitive.

Window

Allen has played more games, so it is fair to ask whether those totals are simply volume. They are not. Per game he still leads, and on the measure that matters most he leads everyone: 2.59 total touchdowns a game over the last six years and 2.53 over the last three, first among qualified quarterbacks in both windows — and first across the full eight years as well. Jackson sits seventh and fourth.

The yardage runs the same direction. Allen averages more total yards a game in both windows, third in the league over six years and fifth over three, against Jackson’s eighth and sixth. Jackson gets more of his on the ground, which is the one place he is genuinely without peer.

The efficiency numbers say the same thing with less volume noise. Over the last six years Allen has run at +0.235 per play to Jackson’s +0.162; over the last three, +0.245 to +0.193. Both windows tell the same story: since Allen’s 2020 breakout he has been the more efficient quarterback and the higher scorer, while Jackson has kept the turnover edge.

Step back and take the six years since 2020 as a whole, and it becomes hard to identify what in these numbers favors Jackson. Allen leads in efficiency, +0.235 to +0.162 per play. He leads in total value, +1005.1 to +540.6. He leads in touchdowns per game, 2.59 to 1.98, and in total yards per game, 291 to 269. He has been on the field more, 98 games to 85.

Jackson leads in exactly two places. He is the far more productive runner, first in the league in rushing yards over the span, and he protects the ball better, 0.82 turnovers a game to 0.98. Those are real edges and they matter. They are not enough to outweigh what sits on the other side of the ledger: the better efficiency, the higher touchdown rate, and the greater yardage.

Act four · season by season

Whose peak is better?

On paper you probably think this is easily Lamar, right? He has two MVPs and three first-team All-Pro selections. Allen has one MVP and two second-team nods. How could Lamar’s peak not be better?

Part of the discussion is that Lamar’s best seasons are that good. 2019 was one of the great quarterback seasons of the modern era. He led the league in both total EPA and EPA per play, at +0.349, and took the MVP unanimously at 22 years old. In 2024 he was outstanding again: +0.316 per play, second in the league, first-team All-Pro. Nothing here diminishes either one of them.

Another part is that the voters simply got 2023 wrong. Jackson’s second MVP came in a season where he finished seventh in EPA per play and sixth in total EPA. Allen was third in both. Jackson accounted for 29 touchdowns that year, tied seventh among quarterbacks, while Allen led the league with 44.

Put that season against every other quarterback MVP since 2001 and Jackson’s 29 touchdowns tie Gannon’s 2002 season for 19th out of 22. Only Steve McNair’s co-MVP and Peyton Manning’s 2008 sit below them. That trophy says more about the story of the season than about who actually played best.

But the real answer is the totality. Pool every season either man has played and rank them: Allen holds six of the top eight by total EPA and five of the top seven by EPA per play. Lamar’s 2019 now leads both lists. Allen’s argument is how many great seasons he has stacked beneath that peak.

His consistency since 2020 has been genuinely remarkable: six straight seasons inside the league’s top ten in total EPA. Go back through the entire play-by-play era, to 1999, and only five other quarterbacks have strung together a run that long — Drew Brees (12 years), Peyton Manning (12), Tom Brady (10), Patrick Mahomes (8 and counting) and Aaron Rodgers (7). That is the company. Is that generally a good list to be on?

Jackson has finished top ten four times in eight years, never more than twice in a row.

The difference is Allen’s historically great consistency.

And if “nerd stats” are not your thing, the traditional numbers hammer the same point home. Rank all sixteen seasons by total touchdowns and Allen holds six of the top eight — exactly the same six-of-eight he holds on total EPA. Rank them by total yards and he holds five of the top eight.

Jackson’s best years hold up fine here too. His 2024 season accounted for 45 touchdowns, one behind Allen’s 46 in 2020, and sits second in yards. But below those, the boards go blue and stay blue.

One more way to frame that consistency, since we are on the subject of touchdowns. Go back through the entire play-by-play era and only two quarterbacks have scored 30 or more total touchdowns in six consecutive seasons: Drew Brees from 2011 to 2016, and Josh Allen from 2020 to 2025.

Allen’s is also the biggest such run anybody has put together. His 254 touchdowns across those six years are the most by any quarterback in any six-year stretch since 1999 — ahead of Brees at 239, Patrick Mahomes at 232, Aaron Rodgers at 222 and Tom Brady at 214. Jackson’s best six, 2019 through 2024, produced 188.

The one caveat is the data itself: play-by-play begins in 1999, so a Dan Marino or a mid-nineties Brett Favre peak sits outside the window.

So whose peak is better? You be the judge. If you like the single best season, or the best two, maybe it’s Lamar. If you like a longer body of work, it seems to pretty clearly favor Josh.

Act five · January

The playoffs, and the wild-card question

Neither of these quarterbacks has played in a Super Bowl. What they do have is twenty-three postseason games between them, and the raw split is the most one-sided comparison on this page: Allen at +0.204 per play across 15 games, Jackson at +0.021 across eight.

Two things complicate that before going further. Jackson’s career number is dragged down by his early playoff struggles. Across the last six years he sits at +0.096 to Allen’s +0.224. Over the last three Jackson improves to +0.176, but Allen is still comfortably ahead at +0.286. That recent Jackson sample is only four games. The other complication is the objection below.

The tale of the tape is about as one-sided as anything on this page. Across their playoff careers Allen beats Jackson on every measure but one. More passing yards per game, 261.0 to 219.1. More touchdowns, 2.60 to 1.62. And half the turnovers, 0.67 a game to 1.38. He has been the better passer, the bigger scorer and the more careful one, all at once.

The exception is rushing yards, where Jackson gains 80.1 a game to Allen’s 51.1. It is a real edge and it has not translated into points.

Across the whole postseason sample the efficiency gap is enormous: +0.204 expected points per play for Allen against +0.021 for Jackson, over 706 playoff snaps and 359. Among the 24 quarterbacks with at least eight playoff games since 1999, Allen ranks 2nd in EPA per play and Jackson 22nd. The gap holds up against a much bigger comparison group.

The consistent counter you hear from the Baltimore crew is that Allen is a wild-card merchant — that Buffalo keeps drawing beatable opponents at home in January and his numbers feast on it.

There is something to it. Allen has been ridiculously good in the wild-card round: +0.266 per play and three touchdowns a game across seven of them. From the divisional round on the level comes down to +0.155 and 2.25 touchdowns. That split is real and worth naming.

League-wide quarterback efficiency from the divisional round onward is +0.101, against +0.066 in the regular season over the same 2018–2025 window. Allen’s +0.155 remains above both. He steps down from extraordinary to very good. And his full postseason rate is higher than his regular-season one, +0.204 to +0.194.

The round-difficulty defense does not explain the whole split. League quarterback efficiency is +0.090 in the wild card and +0.101 from the divisional round on. Those are different groups of teams and players, so this isn’t a controlled test of opponent difficulty. But the league baseline doesn’t fall the way Allen’s individual rate does.

It still doesn’t close the gap, because Jackson falls much further. He goes from +0.163 in the wild card to -0.054 from the divisional round on. In those later rounds Allen leads +0.155 to -0.054 in efficiency, 2.25 touchdowns a game to 1.60, and 0.75 turnovers to 1.60. Among the ten quarterbacks with at least 150 qualifying plays in those rounds since 2018, Allen sits sixth and Jackson tenth.

One fairness point buried in the game counts cuts toward Jackson. Baltimore’s seeding earned byes, so he has played more divisional games than wild-card ones — three wild-card appearances to Allen’s seven. That is precisely why this split is the fair comparison rather than the raw postseason totals. Allen still wins it.

Objection two

“His rushing numbers are a goal-line trick”

The other common chirp about Allen is that his rushing touchdowns are an artifact of usage: park a 250-pound quarterback on the one-yard line enough times and the counting stats take care of themselves.

Two things are wrong with it. The first is that his touchdown runs are not especially short. Since 2021 he has scored 54 times on the ground, and 23 of those came from five yards or further out against 25 from inside two — a 42.6% share from distance, almost exactly the league rate of 43.4%. He is not simply vulturing one-yard plunges.

The sharper test is not how often Buffalo gives him the ball inside the five, but how often it works when they do. Among the 57 players with at least 30 qualifying carries inside the five since 2018, Allen ranks second by success rate. He scores on 62.0% of his attempts against a league rate of 42.6%. His EPA per carry is more than four times the league average.

Jackson is the striking part of this. He ranks 52nd of 57 by success rate in that pool. His 43.3% sits below the league’s 54.0%, and his EPA per carry is negative. The sample is smaller, 30 carries against Allen’s 79, so I’m not treating it as a verdict on every kind of rushing. Jackson is the more dangerous open-field runner. Allen has been much better near the goal line.

So the objection inverts. Allen’s goal-line volume is not padding his numbers; it is the correct call, and it is one of the few places where the gap between them is not close.

It is worth being blunt about how good he is here, because “good for a quarterback” undersells it badly. The two players most associated with short-yardage football over this stretch are Jalen Hurts, whose Eagles built an entire cottage industry out of the tush push, and Derrick Henry, the closest thing the modern league has to a battering ram.

Allen’s 70.9% success rate tops Hurts at 68.8% and Henry at 57.8%. His 62.0% touchdown rate beats Hurts’s 58.4% and Henry’s 48.1%. Hurts does lead in EPA per carry, +0.613 to Allen’s +0.550. Henry sits at +0.276 across 135 carries. Allen belongs in that company without pretending he wins every column.

That is not a quarterback being propped up by his goal-line usage. That is one of the best short-yardage weapons in football, who happens to play quarterback.

And the Buffalo version: is he vulturing James Cook?

There is a Buffalo-specific version of the same complaint, and it is worth answering on its own terms: that Allen vultures touchdowns from James Cook. The word carries an accusation — that carries which belong to the running back are being handed to the quarterback for the sake of his statistics.

Over the last three seasons Buffalo has had 110 qualifying carries from inside the five. Allen took 38% of them and Cook 33%, so the usage split is real enough to argue about. Allen has been more than twenty percentage points better by success rate and roughly seven-tenths of a point better per carry in EPA. Among 38 players with at least twenty carries, Allen ranks 3rd and Cook 23rd.

The gap is widest exactly where the accusation is loudest. From one or two yards out — the sneak range, the carries that look most like poaching — Allen scores on 79% of his attempts and Cook on 53%. Cook's expected points per carry from that distance is negative.

Vulturing implies the touchdown would otherwise have been scored by someone else. The evidence says a meaningful share of them would not have been.

Put it plainly: Josh Allen is flat out better than James Cook in short-yardage situations, and it would be stupid for Buffalo not to leverage that. Handing the ball to the man who converts seven times in ten instead of the one who converts five is not favoritism toward the quarterback. It is just coaching.

The verdict

Where that leaves it

So where does that leave it? If you would rather outsource your opinion to the Mickey Mouse voters at the AP than evaluate the players on their merits, Jackson wins comfortably — two MVPs to one, and three first-team All-Pro selections to none.

Most scholars would agree that Josh Allen’s body of work and consistent excellence make him the better player. Six straight seasons inside the league’s top ten in total EPA, a run only five quarterbacks have matched since 1999. More touchdowns than any quarterback in football in every window since 2020. A postseason record that survives the strongest objection raised against it, and the best short-yardage weapon at the position. And he has been on the field for 126 games to Jackson’s 116.

Lamar Jackson’s case is elite peaks and missed games. It is a real case, and 2019 remains the single best season either of them has produced. It is just not the stronger one.
