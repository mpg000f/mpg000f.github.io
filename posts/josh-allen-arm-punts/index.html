Josh Allen and the Arm Punt

He takes deep chances more often when a failed conversion would likely mean punting. The interception column only captures part of that story.

Salamander Jefferson · October 14, 2026 · Sample: 2022–2026 YTD regular seasons, through October 12

Josh Allen’s interception against the Rams on Monday came on third-and-6 from Buffalo’s own 16 with 37 seconds left before halftime. He sent the ball 44 yards downfield toward Keon Coleman. The Rams intercepted it at their own 40 and returned it three yards.

That is a turnover on the stat sheet. But the Rams started at their own 43 after the pick. An average net punt from Buffalo’s 16 would have put them around their own 42. In terms of the field position Buffalo surrendered, this looked almost identical to the punt that would probably have followed an incomplete pass.

The interception cost Buffalo 1.21 expected points. An incompletion followed by a league-average net punt, under reasonable clock assumptions, would have cost approximately 1.29. The difference was about eight hundredths of an expected point.

Counterfactual assumes a five-second incompletion, a seven-second punt, and 42.2 net punt yards, leaving the Rams around their own 42 with 25 seconds remaining. This is an estimate, not an observed sequence; modest changes to time and punt distance can reverse the tiny difference.

Why the argument is worth researching

The phrase arm punt has become its own argument on Twitter. Search the term and the debate is easy to find. Calling a pick an arm punt can sound like making excuses for Allen, and plenty of people reject the idea that a long third-down interception could be no worse than an incompletion followed by a punt.

But in this specific case, that is almost exactly what the field position and EPA calculation show. The Rams got the ball in essentially the same place, at a similar point before halftime. The word interception does not make those two resulting situations fundamentally different.

That is the case for giving Keon Coleman a shot at the jump ball. A catch converts the third down and gives Buffalo a chance to keep driving. A defensive pass-interference penalty can do the same. A deep interception without a substantial return can leave the Rams near the field position they would have received after a punt.

It is still possible to criticize the read, the throw or an earlier missed opportunity. The point is that a contested deep shot can have a different risk-reward tradeoff once the safer conversion is no longer available. I wanted to know whether Allen takes these chances more often than other quarterbacks, and whether the caught interceptions tell the whole story.

The premise: the safe conversion is gone

The arm-punt argument rests on a specific premise: the play is effectively dead, with no safe, realistic way left to pick up the first down. If the quarterback throws it away or the ball falls incomplete, the team is going to punt. The choice at that point is whether to accept that ending or give a receiver a chance to win a difficult ball or draw defensive pass interference.

Under that premise, a risky downfield throw can be worth taking because it offers a route to keeping the possession alive, while some interception outcomes resemble the punt already waiting on fourth down. The return risk still matters. A deep pick returned a long way is a different outcome from one caught and stopped near the receiving team’s own goal line.

If a safer first-down conversion is available, the premise breaks down. An open receiver beyond the sticks or a realistic scramble for the first down is a better alternative than a low-percentage heave. Our punt-likelihood model does not see those options; confirming that the safe conversion was gone requires film. The data can identify situations consistent with this strategy, not certify every throw as the right decision.

Putting a definition around the arm punt

I wanted to test that idea without calling every third-down interception an arm punt. A short pick returned into scoring territory can be disastrous, even when the offense would otherwise have punted. Throw depth matters, and so does the likelihood that the team would actually punt after an incompletion.

A probable arm-punt interception must meet all four conditions:
  1. It happens on third down.
  2. After an incompletion, the estimated probability of punting is at least 80%.
  3. There are at least 15 seconds remaining in the half before the snap.
  4. The pass travels at least expected net punt distance from that field position minus 10 yards.

Returns do not change the classification. They change the cost of the play. Fourth-down interceptions are excluded. This is a working definition for this analysis, not an established NFL statistic or a claim that the quarterback intended to throw an interception.

The distance rule accounts for where the offense is on the field. A typical punt from deep in your own territory gains more field position than one from the opponent’s side of midfield. A single fixed throw-depth threshold would miss that distinction.

For the Rams play, Buffalo’s expected net punt was approximately 43 yards. The minimum qualifying throw depth was therefore about 33 yards. Allen’s 44-yard throw comfortably qualifies.

The box score misses some of the risk

A defender can drop a dangerous pass. A receiver can win the ball despite an interception opportunity. If I count only the passes that were actually intercepted, I am partly measuring whether the defense finished the play.

FTN Data via nflverse provides a public, play-level interception-worthy flag from 2022 onward. That lets me extend the analysis to probable arm-punt risk throws: passes meeting the same situation and depth criteria that were either intercepted or charted as interception-worthy. A pass meeting both conditions counts once.

That flag is broader than a confirmed dropped interception. It describes a risky throw, including one that ultimately became a completion. I therefore keep the caught interceptions and additional charted risks separate in the counts.

Allen takes the deep shot more often

The main comparison runs from the 2022 regular season through October 12, 2026, including the current season’s first five weeks. The 2026 season is partial, and the rates pool passing opportunities rather than averaging seasons. It applies the same criteria to Allen and every other quarterback, excludes non-QB trick-play passes, and uses pass attempts rather than sacks or scrambles as the denominator. Every third-down attempt in the comparison matched an FTN charting record.

Allen had 284 attempts in third-down situations where an incompletion would likely lead to a punt. Other quarterbacks combined for 10,621. This is a pooled, attempt-weighted league baseline with Allen removed.

Allen attempted a pass reaching the arm-punt distance threshold on 32 of those 284 throws, or 11.3%. The rest of the league did it on 574 of 10,621, or 5.4%.

That is approximately 2.1 times the league frequency. Before looking at who caught the ball, Allen was already choosing these deep opportunities more often.

The risky outcomes are more frequent, too

Six of Allen’s qualifying throws were intercepted. Three additional throws were charted as interception-worthy without being intercepted. That produces nine qualifying risk throws across the sample.

For other quarterbacks, the corresponding counts were 35 interceptions and 28 additional interception-worthy throws, or 63 total.

Allen produced a qualifying risky throw on 3.17% of these attempts, compared with 0.59% for everyone else. That is approximately five times the rate.

It is not entirely a consequence of attempting more deep passes. Among the deep throws themselves, nine of Allen’s 32 were intercepted or interception-worthy: 28.1%. The league figure was 63 of 574: 11.0%.

Using FTN’s interception-worthy flags alone barely changes the comparison: all nine Allen plays remain, while the league count falls from 63 to 61.

The nine plays behind the finding

The intercepted passes are familiar examples of the arm-punt idea. Allen’s 65-yard interception against the Jets in 2023 left New York at its own 4. His 52-yard interception against Jacksonville that season left the Jaguars at their own 7. Those plays carried very different consequences from a short interception returned into the red zone.

A second example: give Diggs a chance

The 2023 Jacksonville game provides an even clearer field-position example. Buffalo faced third-and-15 at its own 41 early in the fourth quarter. Allen sent a deep ball toward Stefon Diggs, giving his receiver a chance to win a contested catch. Darious Williams won the battle, and Jacksonville took possession at its own 7, with no return.

Counterfactual uses a five-second incompletion and a seven-second punt, leaving 13:04 in the fourth quarter versus 13:06 after the actual pick. It preserves timeouts and the game’s neutral-site, outdoor setting. Net distance is the same field-position model used in our qualifying criteria.

The interception was more favorable than that punt alternative by approximately 0.65 expected points. That is the football logic behind giving Diggs a chance: if he wins the ball or draws a penalty, Buffalo can extend the possession. In the actual losing outcome of the catch contest, Jacksonville still received the ball farther back than a typical punt would have put it.

That does not establish that every other option was closed or that this was the optimal throw. It establishes the narrower, useful point: the interception’s immediate field-position cost was not worse than failing to convert and punting. The EPA comparison evaluates those resulting states, not what Jacksonville eventually did on its drive.

What the Rams interception adds

The Monday-night interception is included in the headline comparison. Within 2026 alone, Allen has one qualifying risky throw in 23 likely-punt third-down attempts; other quarterbacks have three in 730. That partial-season sample is too small for a standalone conclusion. The full 2022–2025 window gives the same broad result: roughly twice the deep-shot frequency and five times the qualifying risk rate. Adding 2026 does not create the finding.

More importantly, the play illustrates the reasoning behind the hypothesis. Allen had an opportunity to convert a third down with a deep pass. If the ball was incomplete, Buffalo would almost certainly punt. If it was intercepted well downfield without a major return, the resulting field position could look similar to that punt. In this instance, it did.

What I take from it

The data supports a specific interpretation of Allen’s aggression. When the fallback after an incompletion is likely to be a punt, he attempts these deep shots more often than other quarterbacks. Those throws also produce intercepted or interception-worthy outcomes more often, even after accounting for his greater willingness to attempt them.

Allen’s risk-taking has context: the fallback after a failed conversion can make some deep interception risk less costly.

The interpretation is strongest when film shows the safe conversion is gone. The numbers do not prove that condition on each play, or that Allen consciously changes his decision-making because he is thinking about an arm punt. It also does not show that the extra aggression is optimal. Establishing that would require comparing the upside from completions and penalties, the downside from returns, and the alternatives he passed up.

What it does show is that the interception column leaves out two useful distinctions: where and when the risk was taken, and whether the defense actually secured the ball. Allen’s profile looks different when both are considered.

Sources and methodology

Sources: nflverse play-by-play, FTN Data via nflverse, and the nflverse player registry. FTN charting is provided under CC BY-SA 4.0; attribution belongs to FTN Data via nflverse. The accompanying FTN-derived data is shared under the same license.

Sample: 2022–2026 YTD NFL regular seasons, with current-year play-by-play and FTN charting through October 12. The four complete seasons and the partial 2026 season are pooled by attempts, not equally weighted by season. Only registered quarterbacks are included. Sacks, scrambles, designed runs, spikes, kneel-downs, two-point attempts and nullified plays are excluded from passing opportunities. Fourth downs are excluded. FTN joins use game and play IDs and have full coverage of the included third-down attempts.

Punt likelihood: a custom gradient-boosted classifier trained on observed fourth-down punt, field-goal and go-for-it decisions from 2021–2025. Features are field position, yards to go, quarter, half/game time remaining, score differential, both teams’ timeouts and home status. For an incomplete third-down pass, the hypothetical fourth-down state keeps field position and distance and advances the clock five seconds. Historical seasons are scored with that season excluded from training; 2026 is scored using 2021–2025. This estimates league behavior, not an optimal recommendation or Buffalo-specific coaching intent.

Distance threshold: a separate model estimates net punt distance from field position, using nonblocked, penalty-free punts from 2021–2025 and accounting for touchbacks. A throw qualifies if its air yards are at least that expected distance minus 10. Air yards describe throw depth before the interception return; the return is not used to classify risk.

Risk label: actual interception OR FTN is_interception_worthy, counted once. A charted risky throw need not be an incompletion or a confirmed dropped interception. The league baseline pools attempts across all other QBs rather than averaging individual quarterback percentages.

Limits: the thresholds were developed during exploratory analysis of Allen’s plays, not preregistered. There are only nine qualifying Allen risk throws in the main sample. Identical rules and opportunity normalization improve comparability, but do not exactly match every game situation or control for receivers, pressure, coverage and coaching preferences. The results describe a tendency; they do not establish intent, causation or strategic optimality.
