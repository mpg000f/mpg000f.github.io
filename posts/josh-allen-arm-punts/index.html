Josh Allen and the Arm Punt

Does Allen take more deep risks when the alternative is a punt? And have those interceptions actually cost Buffalo more than punting would?

Salamander Jefferson · October 14, 2026 · Sample: 2022–2026 YTD regular seasons, through October 12
Jump to:Twitter debateDefinitionAllen vs. other QBsWhat the picks costOther examplesShort of the sticks

Josh Allen’s interception against the Rams on Monday came on third-and-6 from Buffalo’s own 16 with 37 seconds left before halftime. He sent the ball 44 yards downfield toward Keon Coleman. The Rams intercepted it at their own 40 and returned it three yards.

That is a turnover on the stat sheet. But the Rams started at their own 43 after the pick. A typical net punt from Buffalo’s 16 would have put them around their own 41. In terms of the field position Buffalo surrendered, this looked almost identical to the punt that would probably have followed an incomplete pass.

The interception cost Buffalo 1.21 expected points. An incompletion followed by the expected net punt from that field position, under reasonable clock assumptions, would have cost approximately 1.28. The difference was about seven hundredths of an expected point. For a single play, 0.07 EPA is negligible. It reflects roughly two yards of field position and a few seconds on the clock when the Rams take over on first down.

This is an estimate, not an observed sequence; modest changes to time and punt distance can reverse the tiny difference.

Why the argument is worth researching

The phrase arm punt has become its own argument on Twitter. Search the term and the debate is easy to find. Here at The Eye Test, we like to run the numbers, so we decided to do a deep dive.

Calling a pick an arm punt can sound like making excuses for Allen, and plenty of people reject the idea that a long third-down interception could be no worse than an incompletion followed by a punt.

But the Rams play shows why that comparison is worth taking seriously. Giving Keon Coleman a shot at the jump ball offered Buffalo a chance to keep the drive alive through a catch or defensive pass interference. When the gamble failed, the interception and short return left the Rams almost exactly where an incompletion and punt would have put them.

Obviously, completing the pass to an open receiver and keeping the drive alive is better in these situations. The comparison we are interested in is with the fallback after failing to convert. But it raises an intriguing question: does Allen accept more downfield interception risk when a possession is likely to end with a punt anyway?

This article focuses on how often Allen takes these risks and what the resulting interceptions cost compared with punting. We are not measuring the full upside of the deep shots or trying to prove that this strategy is optimal.

We wanted to answer two questions: does Allen take more deep risks than other quarterbacks when an incompletion would likely lead to a punt? And how much have his qualifying interceptions cost Buffalo compared with that fallback?

The hypothesis is that Allen accepts more risk because the downside can be smaller in these situations. The data supports that tendency: he takes the deep shot about twice as often, and those deep passes are intercepted or marked interception-worthy on about five times the share of likely-punt third-down attempts. The outcomes of his six qualifying interceptions also cost Buffalo less in total EPA than the modeled punts would have. That gives the aggression some context.

The premise: the safe conversion is gone

The arm-punt argument rests on a specific premise: the play is effectively dead, with no safe, realistic way left to pick up the first down. If the quarterback throws it away or the ball falls incomplete, the team is going to punt. The choice at that point is whether to accept that ending or give a receiver a chance to win a difficult ball or draw defensive pass interference.

If an open receiver or a realistic scramble can get the first down, take it. The EPA model cannot tell us whether those options existed. That takes film. This analysis tests the pattern and the cost of the resulting picks.

Putting a definition around the arm punt

We wanted to test that idea without calling every third-down interception an arm punt. A short pick returned into scoring territory can be disastrous, even when the offense would otherwise have punted. Throw depth matters, and so does the likelihood that the team would actually punt after an incompletion.

A probable arm-punt interception must meet all four conditions:
  1. It happens on third down.
  2. After an incompletion, the estimated probability of punting is at least 80%.
  3. There are at least 15 seconds remaining in the half before the snap.
  4. The throw reaches roughly punt distance: no more than 10 yards short of the expected net punt. If we expect a 43-yard punt, the pass must travel at least 33 yards downfield.

Returns do not change the classification. They change the cost of the play. Fourth-down interceptions are excluded. This is a working definition for this analysis, not an established NFL statistic or a claim that the quarterback intended to throw an interception.

Punt distance depends on field position, so the throw-depth requirement does too.

For the Rams play, Buffalo’s expected net punt was approximately 43 yards. The minimum qualifying throw depth was therefore about 33 yards. Allen’s 44-yard throw comfortably qualifies.

Counting the risk, including balls the defense did not catch

Counting interceptions alone misses dangerous passes that a defender drops or a receiver wins. FTN Data via nflverse provides an interception-worthy flag from 2022 onward. We count a qualifying deep pass as a risk throw if it was intercepted or received that flag, counting each play once.

The flag is broader than a dropped interception and can include a completion. We keep the caught picks and additional charted risky throws separate below.

Allen takes the deep shot more often

The data runs from the 2022 regular season through October 12, 2026, including the current season’s first five weeks. Every QB gets the same criteria, and the rates use pass attempts, excluding sacks and scrambles. All included attempts matched FTN charting. The full rules are in the methodology below.

Allen had 284 attempts in third-down situations where an incompletion would likely lead to a punt.

Allen attempted a pass reaching the arm-punt distance threshold on 32 of those 284 throws, or 11.3%. The rest of the league did it on 574 of 10,621, or 5.4%.

This shows Josh Allen likes to chuck it deep on third down when the fallback is likely to be a punt.

That is 2.1 times the league frequency.

The risky outcomes are more frequent, too

Six of Allen’s qualifying throws were intercepted. Three additional throws were charted as interception-worthy without being intercepted. That produces nine qualifying risk throws across the sample.

For other quarterbacks, the corresponding counts were 35 interceptions and 28 additional interception-worthy throws, or 63 total.

It is not entirely a consequence of attempting more deep passes. Among the deep throws themselves, nine of Allen’s 32 were intercepted or interception-worthy: 28.1%. The league figure was 63 of 574: 11.0%.

The picks-only comparison is stronger: Allen was intercepted on 6 of 284 likely-punt attempts (2.11%), versus 35 of 10,621 (0.33%) for other QBs, about six times the rate. This suggests Josh Allen is more willing to put the ball in harm’s way to give his receiver a chance to make a play. You can argue about whether that is smart. The pattern is consistent with accepting more risk in these situations.

What happened across the whole interception sample?

The Rams and Jaguars examples explain the idea, but examples alone can cherry-pick the answer. So We evaluated all six of Allen’s qualifying interceptions and all 35 from other quarterbacks in the 2022–2026 YTD sample. Returns did not determine inclusion. A qualifying pick-six would remain in the sample and count fully against the quarterback; none of these 41 picks was returned for a touchdown.

For each pick, We compared its recorded EPA with the combined EPA of an incompletion and a typical punt from that field position. Both comparisons stop when the opponent takes possession. The calculation does not depend on what happened on the opponent’s next drive.

Allen’s actual interceptions totaled −3.52 EPA. Failing to convert and taking the modeled punts would have totaled −5.95 EPA. The observed picks were therefore +2.43 EPA better in total, or +0.40 per interception. They still cost Buffalo expected points; they simply cost fewer than that fallback estimate.

Some of Allen’s favorable results may be return luck. His qualifying picks have not been overly costly to the Bills compared with punting, but across the rest of the league these interceptions averaged 0.45 EPA worse than the modeled punt fallback. Allen has avoided the damaging returns that can make this gamble expensive. If ball placement, receiver choice or the matchup lets a quarterback take these chances while limiting return opportunities, there may be a skill worth studying here. Here at The Eye Test, we are not going to study the film. We’ll leave that to the Film Crew.

Allen has had good fortune with the results of these arm punts so far. That does not mean it will continue. One long interception return could change the picture quickly.

This is not a Josh Allen invention

The underlying idea is much older than Allen. The broccoli-haired football watchers acting like this concept was invented yesterday ought to read a book. In 1988, Bob Carroll, Pete Palmer and John Thorn published The Hidden Game of Football, an analytics book that challenged conventional football wisdom by examining the value of game situations. See this Athletic article for a breakdown on some of the topics they cover.

The arm-punt discussion follows the same principle: judge the resulting situation, rather than assuming the interception label tells the whole story. And the on-field examples extend well beyond Buffalo.

Patrick Mahomes had a nasty arm punt against Houston last December. On third-and-six from Kansas City’s own 42, he threw 42 yards downfield. The interception left Houston at its own 16. Our model estimated a punt was very likely after an incompletion, and the throw clears the same depth threshold used for Allen. The play qualifies under our definition.

The other choice: throwing short of the sticks

There is another side to this argument: what are quarterbacks doing instead of taking the deep shot? A safe completion that leaves the offense punting still ends the drive. We are more sympathetic to giving a receiver a chance than padding a completion percentage. But we should check whether those short throws actually convert before calling anyone a checkdown artist.

But the data needs two separate labels. Short of the sticks means the pass traveled fewer air yards than the yards needed for the first down. That includes screens and throws close enough to convert with yards after the catch. A charted checkdown is a throw FTN explicitly labeled CHK in the quarterback’s progression. Those categories overlap, but they are not interchangeable. FTN’s dictionary explicitly separates checkdowns from designed reads such as screens.

Allen is at the bottom, but he has company: Stafford, Jackson and Purdy are all around 9%, while Mahomes is at 10.3%. These are FTN charted checkdowns, not every throw short of the sticks. A low checkdown rate alone does not tell us whether the quarterback made the best decision.

Allen threw short of the sticks on 35.2% of these attempts, versus 45.0% for other QBs. FTN marked 23 of 284 attempts as checkdowns (8.1%), the lowest rate among the 39 qualified QBs.

Checkdowns were recorded in 2022, including five for Allen. Excluding that year, Allen is still second-lowest at 8.3%, behind Purdy at 8.0%, using a 100-attempt minimum in the shorter window. The full coding audit is linked in the methodology.

What we take from it

Allen likes to chuck it deep more than most when the fallback is a punt. He does it about twice as often as the rest of the league, and he puts the ball at risk more often even among those deep throws. Love and Mahomes take these chances too, but no qualified QB puts the ball at risk on deep third-down throws more often in these likely-punt situations.

The outcomes of his six qualifying picks were 2.43 EPA better in total than the modeled incompletion-and-punt fallback. In other words, those six results were a net positive for Buffalo compared with the expected results of punting on the next play. That is an advantage over the fallback, not a claim that the interceptions themselves added points.

Some of that comes down to how the interceptions were returned, and there is luck in those outcomes. A long return or a better hypothetical punt can change the comparison. But the arm punts Allen has actually thrown in this sample have not really cost the Bills extra compared with failing to convert and punting.

The fallback matters. A deep interception can be a bad result for the pass and still leave the defense in roughly the same spot as a punt.

We think that gives Allen’s aggression a reasonable explanation. When the safe conversion is gone, give the receiver a shot. The tradeoff is the chance of a catch or DPI against the risk of an interception and a long return. Who you are throwing to matters.

Treating every interception as equally damaging ignores the situation that prompted the throw and the field position it actually surrendered.

Sources, methodology and limits

Sources: nflverse play-by-play, nflverse schedules and venue metadata, FTN Data via nflverse, and the nflverse player registry. FTN charting is provided under CC BY-SA 4.0; attribution belongs to FTN Data via nflverse. The accompanying FTN-derived data is shared under the same license.

Sample: 2022–2026 YTD NFL regular seasons, with current-year play-by-play and FTN charting through October 12. The four complete seasons and the partial 2026 season are pooled by attempts, not equally weighted by season. Only registered quarterbacks are included. Sacks, scrambles, designed runs, spikes, kneel-downs, two-point attempts and nullified plays are excluded from passing opportunities. Fourth downs are excluded. FTN joins use game and play IDs and have full coverage of the included third-down attempts.

Punt likelihood: a custom gradient-boosted classifier trained on observed fourth-down punt, field-goal and go-for-it decisions from 2021–2025. Features are field position, yards to go, quarter, half/game time remaining, score differential, both teams’ timeouts and home status. For an incomplete third-down pass, the hypothetical fourth-down state keeps field position and distance and advances the clock five seconds. Historical seasons are scored with that season excluded from training; 2026 is scored using 2021–2025. This estimates league behavior, not an optimal recommendation or Buffalo-specific coaching intent.

Distance threshold: a separate model estimates net punt distance from field position, using nonblocked, penalty-free punts from 2021–2025 and accounting for touchbacks. A throw qualifies if its air yards are at least that expected distance minus 10. Air yards describe throw depth before the interception return; the return is not used to classify risk.

Risk label: actual interception OR FTN is_interception_worthy, counted once. A charted risky throw need not be an incompletion or a confirmed dropped interception. The league baseline pools attempts across all other QBs rather than averaging individual quarterback percentages.

Historical context: University of Chicago Press verifies the 1988 publication of The Hidden Game of Football by Bob Carroll, Pete Palmer and John Thorn; the linked Athletic retrospective provides further historical context.

Video and debate links: the Twitter/X search, B/R Gridiron’s Rams clip, and the official Jaguars clip. The Rams preview opens the original X post because that source video disables off-site embedding. The Jaguars preview links to the official team player. The Mahomes clip similarly opens the original X post; the Maye highlight links to the Bills’ official player. Highlight Heaven’s compilation uses YouTube’s standard embedded player with a direct watch link. No video files are rehosted.

Rams counterfactual: evaluated using the nflfastR expected-points model. The two-play EPA is measured from Buffalo’s original third-down state to the Rams’ possession after the hypothetical punt. The assumed clock, net distance and timeouts are stated beside the comparison.

Jaguars counterfactual: the same EP model reproduces the recorded pre-snap EP and post-interception state. The assumed 42.4-yard net punt leaves Jacksonville near its own 17 rather than its own 7. Holding timeouts fixed and advancing the clock 12 seconds gives approximately −0.46 combined EPA, versus +0.19 for the actual interception. Exact inputs and values are in the linked counterfactual audit.

Individual QBs and checkdowns: rankings require 100 likely-punt third-down attempts in the same period. Short-of-sticks means air yards strictly below yards to gain; it includes designed passes and any recorded throwaways. FTN read_thrown = CHK identifies charted checkdowns and uses all qualifying attempts as its denominator. Blank reads are not assumed to be checkdowns. Conversion means a recorded first down or touchdown, including yards after the catch. The QB audit includes opportunity counts, distance to gain, missing depth and a throwaway-excluded sensitivity. The validation audit confirms checkdowns were recorded in 2022; excluding that season leaves Allen second-lowest among QBs with at least 100 eligible attempts from 2023 onward. Threshold robustness uses frozen predictions and all nine combinations of 70/80/90% punt probability and 5/10/15-yard depth shortfalls. The short-pass outcome audit additionally counts official completions and first-down/touchdown conversions, including conversions on noncompleted attempts such as accepted penalties. Rates for completed throws use completed short passes as denominator. The main comparison excludes Allen from the other-QB baseline.

Full interception outcome audit: all six qualifying Allen picks and 35 other-QB picks are evaluated against a five-second incompletion and seven-second punt. The net-distance model is held constant across QBs; it uses nonblocked, penalty-free 2021–2025 punts. The receiving EP is evaluated with first-and-10, the relevant roof and home/neutral status, and swapped possession timeouts. Combined punt EPA equals negative receiving EP minus the original EP. The intermediate fourth-down EP cancels. Immediate post-interception states retain nullified rows so later penalties do not contaminate field position; both the pre-snap model EP and actual EPA/post-state identity are verified for all 41 plays. Exact per-play inputs, results and sensitivities are downloadable; analysis/evaluate_outcomes.py reproduces them. Values use a typical punt state rather than the average EP across a full punt-outcome distribution.

Limits: the thresholds were developed during exploratory analysis of Allen’s plays, not preregistered. There are only nine qualifying Allen risk throws in the main sample. Identical rules and opportunity normalization improve comparability, but do not exactly match every game situation or control for receivers, pressure, coverage and coaching preferences. The results describe a tendency and the cost of six observed interceptions; they do not establish intent or strategic optimality. A likely punt after an incompletion does not establish that all short options were unavailable. We deliberately do not measure the full upside of the 32 deep attempts here.
